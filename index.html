Sprigs

A sprig is not scripted. It has a body full of chemicals, a small brain that learns from how it feels, and a genome that decides how both are wired. Nothing it does was written down in advance.

Looking after one

  • Point at something to draw a sprig's attention to it — and to teach it that thing's name.
  • Tickle to praise. Whatever it was just doing, it will do more of. Scold to discourage. Both work through real chemistry, so they take a few seconds to sink in.
  • Carry to pick things up — including sprigs. Put a cold one by the lamp, or two grown ones together.
  • Say a word from the bar. Say it while a sprig can see what you mean and it will slowly work out what the word refers to. Eventually it will use the word back at you.

The valley

Berries and bushes are food. Pools are water. The lamp is warm, the grotto in the west is cold. The white-flowered herb cures illness. The purple weeds out past the lamp are poisonous — a sprig has to learn that the hard way, and it will remember.

Growing up

Sprigs pass through baby, child, adolescent, adult and elder. Two adults who spend time together may lay an egg, and the hatchling's genome is a genuine mix of theirs with mutations of its own. Colour, shape, appetite, temper and how quickly it learns are all inherited.
